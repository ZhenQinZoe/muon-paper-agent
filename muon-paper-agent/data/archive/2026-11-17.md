## Top Highlights

1. High-dimensional limit theorems for adaptive variants of SGD I: Ballistic limits
Authors: Aukosh Jagannath, Varnan Sarangian
Link: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19059v1
Category: Muon Core
Paper type: Theory
Relevance score: 13.0
Abstract: We develop the high-dimensional effective dynamics for adaptive, momentum-based variants of Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD). These effective dynamics are given by a Markov process. They key observation is that the non-Markovianity in the algorithm due to the exponential moving average and preconditioning can be treated using the method of averaging. Our approach applies to a broad class of algorithms including to Adaptive momentum estimation (ADAM), Momentum orthogonalized by Newton-Schultz (MUON), and its variants. It also applies to a broad class of learning tasks, such at tensor PCA, Gaussian projective models, teacher-student networks, and a multi-layer neural network trained on the XOR problem using a hybrid of MUON and ADAM as commonly done in practice. We demonstrate how to this technique can give precise, quantitative comparisons between algorithms. In particular, we find interesting phenomena, such as the cross-over phenomenon where online SGD has better generalization than ADAM early in training until the learning curves cross and ADAM dominates. Furthermore, we prove a version of the Edge of Stability phenomenon. In particular, we introduce an analogue of the infinity Laplacian that sharply characterizes the Edge of Stability.

2. Grokking Can Be Steered: Selecting Desirable Solutions By Choice Of Learning Rule
Authors: Pascal Sager, Benjamin Grewe, Pau Vilimelis Aceituno, Thilo Stadelmann
Link: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19477v1
Category: Muon-Adjacent Experiments
Paper type: Experiment
Relevance score: 10.0
Abstract: During grokking, a network transitions from memorization to generalization, yet what shapes the neural implementation (circuit) of the generalizing solution and specifically the number of participating neurons (its size) remains unclear. We find that the learning rule, the combination of credit assignment algorithm and optimizer, controls this: On modular addition, every learning rule we study that groks recovers the same Fourier-based algorithm, yet implemented by circuits that differ in size. Why? A neuron stays in use when its credit is large relative to the non-gradient term of its own update, and this single condition unifies three routes to unused neurons: the coupled weight decay, Adam's $ε$ and the decay of a trainable per-neuron gain. Equalizing the magnitude of credit assigned to each neuron, or scaling each neuron's weight decay by its credit, expands the effective circuit size, measured from the outgoing weights, from 60% to 99% of the layer's neurons at similar training and test accuracy. We vary which neurons share one equalization factor, from the whole layer to individual neurons, and find that the circuit stays concentrated for groups of eight or more and fully distributes only when every neuron has its own factor. Credit magnitudes are usually set by the normalization of standard optimizers: Nero, which normalizes per neuron, distributes the computation, whereas Adam and seven others, including RMSProp, Muon, and Shampoo, use other normalization schemes and retain small, concentrated circuits. Distributed circuits tolerate neuron deletion better, while concentrated circuits can be pruned further and transfer about twice as fast to a task that recombines the same features, largely by recruiting their unused neurons. Learning rule choice thus steers circuit size, trading robustness against compressibility and transfer speed without changing the learned algorithm.

3. Do Pretraining Optimizer Gains Transfer to KL Distillation?
Authors: Lavinia Ghita, Dhruv Desai
Link: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19419v1
Category: Muon-Adjacent Experiments
Paper type: Experiment
Relevance score: 10.0
Abstract: Matrix-structured optimizers can improve language-model pretraining, but it is unclear whether these gains transfer to short-horizon KL distillation from an instruction-tuned initialization. We compare AdamW and diagonal AdaGrad with two tractable Shampoo reductions, SOAP, and All-2D Muon across four dense Qwen student-teacher pairs under a common forward-KL objective and fixed training budget. Across scales, none of the evaluated matrix-structured recipes consistently improves the quality versus trainer-time trade-off over the elementwise references: AdamW generally attains the lowest validation KL, while AdaGrad can be slightly faster at larger scale. SOAP often tracks AdamW in KL but incurs substantial trainer-time overhead, and the two Shampoo reductions reverse ordering across model pairs, showing that conclusions depend on the feasible recipe. These results suggest that pretraining optimizer gains do not transfer automatically to KL distillation and should be re-established for the target objective and systems regime.

4. Emergent Steering Beyond Endpoint Alignment in Chemical Reaction Models
Authors: Yili Shen, Kehan Guo, Haomin Zhuang, Xiangliang Zhang
Link: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21423v1
Category: Not Relevant
Paper type: Experiment
Relevance score: 8.0
Abstract: Machine learning models for chemical reaction prediction are usually evaluated by endpoint accuracy, but endpoint correctness alone does not reveal how a model represents chemical transformation or why it fails. We introduce UniRxnAxis, a unified forward-retrosynthesis framework that models both tasks as direction-conditioned endpoint redistribution in a shared latent space. By aligning source states, transformed states, and encoded target endpoints, UniRxnAxis enables directional auditing of learned latent updates. This shared latent geometry allows the learned displacement (from the encoder source state $S_0$ to the decoder-facing transformed state $S_1$) to be decomposed into an endpoint-parallel component and an endpoint-orthogonal residual, which we call emergent steering. Our evaluation shows that emergent steering is functionally important: in retrosynthesis, it recovers most of the model's utility, remains effective under random, shuffled, and prototype controls, and directly affects which bond edits are promoted or suppressed. The residual-over-parallel ordering persists across alignment strengths, after decoder-interface calibration, against angle-matched random axes, and in a second graph-edit architecture, although its magnitude depends on the reference axis and optimization. The decomposition also supports a proxy taxonomy of model-side failures, showing how reaction models can be audited beyond endpoint accuracy.

5. CertiSum: Bitwise-Identical MXFP4 Training Across Platforms and Parallelism Layouts
Authors: Steve Rhyner, Yubo Gao, Stefan de Lasa, Sankeerth Durvasula, Nandita Vijaykumar
Link: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21584v1
Category: Not Relevant
Paper type: Experiment
Relevance score: 5.5
Abstract: Training with MXFP4, a 4-bit microscaling format, is non-deterministic: identical inputs can produce different results across parallelism layouts and GPU vendors. These differences complicate debugging and training audits and can cause training and inference engines to disagree. We present CertiSum to make MXFP4 training bitwise invariant across runs, parallelism layouts, and GPU vendors. Its key insight is that native FP32 accumulation of MXFP4 products is often already exact for every summation order. CertiSum collects statistics during quantization to certify native matrix multiplication results, recomputes uncertified outputs exactly, and uses exact kernels directly where certification rarely succeeds. CertiSum keeps quantization blocks unchanged across layouts and merges partial sums without rounding, including in attention and weight-gradient computation. In 200-step Qwen3-8B-Base experiments, CertiSum preserves training states bit for bit across AMD MI350X and NVIDIA GB200 and across changes in data, tensor, and context parallelism on MI350X. Across three 8B--14B models on FineWeb-Edu using MI350X, it adds 11.8--60.5% to training-step latency relative to its non-exact MXFP4 counterpart at the larger evaluated input shapes, with speedups of up to $1.24\times$ over BF16 and $1.70\times$ over deterministic BF16.
